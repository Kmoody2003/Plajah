# The Living Combat Atlas — capture programme and rights posture

*A proposal from Plajah · Draft for review — not yet circulated*

---

## 1. What already exists

The Living Combat Atlas is a digital museum of world martial arts inside
Plajah's academic section. It is built, not proposed.

**Fifty accessions across five wings** — African (17), Asian (12), European (9),
Arts of the Americas and Diaspora (8), Oceanic and Pacific (4). Each carries a
lede, history, technique, ritual and cultural frame, documented masters or
lineages, diaspora connections, and cited sources.

**A rights-cleared media layer.** Roughly 250 images and 100 documentary films,
every one public domain, CC0, or Creative Commons attribution — with licence and
photographer credit rendered on screen beside the image, not buried in a footer.
Every URL, video identifier and source was verified at the point of curation.

**Its own holdings.** All four volumes of Newberry's *Beni Hasan* survey
(Egypt Exploration Fund, 1893–1900) and 86 CC0 objects from the Metropolitan
Museum are ingested into our own storage — 302 MB, 94 assets, each with a
machine-readable rights record carrying licence, attribution and verification
date. The museum serves its own copies and cites the source, rather than
hotlinking other institutions' servers.

**A plate room.** The Beni Hasan wrestling walls — Tomb 15 (Baqet III) and
Tomb 17 (Khety) — extracted from our own scans and served at scan resolution.
Each register renders as a filmstrip the visitor scrolls left to right, so
individual holds are legible. At whole-plate scale they are specks; this is the
only way the four-thousand-year-old sequence can actually be read.

**A motion lab.** A browser player that streams real capture data through a
forward-kinematics BVH parser: four CMU Graphics Lab fighting sequences, free
for any use, plus one UMONS-TAICHI Kinect sequence displayed as a labelled
research preview pending permission.

## 2. What is missing, everywhere

There is no motion dataset — skeletal or volumetric — of Dambe, Laamb, Engolo,
Nguni stick fighting, Musangwe, Moraingy, Evala, Nuba wrestling, Kokawa, Mgba,
or the Lutte Traditionnelle family.

Not a limited one. None.

The written record exists, largely through T. J. Desch-Obi's *Fighting for
Honor*. The video record exists but is scattered across festival footage and
broadcast documentary. The kinetic record has never been made. The Hong Kong
Martial Arts Living Archive proved the method on Southern Chinese arts; it has
not, to our knowledge, been applied to African or diasporic combat systems.

The first institution to make that record does not join a field. It founds one.

## 3. The capture programme

Three tiers, in ascending cost and ambition. Tier 1 is running now.

**Tier 1 — Video-to-motion.** Own-shot or licensed multi-angle video passed
through GVHMR-class reconstruction into BVH, then into the Motion Lab player.
Near-zero hardware. First targets: Taijiquan, validated against UMONS-TAICHI as
ground truth, then Dambe and Laamb from commissioned field video. Rights caveat
we hold ourselves to: motion derived from someone else's footage inherits that
footage's questions, so we use own-shot or explicitly licensed video only.

**Tier 2 — Field capture.** Inertial-suit or markerless multi-camera capture on
location. Produces clean skeletal data for technique libraries and for
teacher-versus-student comparison — the skill-scoring dimension UMONS-TAICHI
demonstrated, applied to arts that have never been measured.

**Tier 3 — Volumetric.** Portable multi-camera rigs and 4D Gaussian Splatting,
giving free-viewpoint playback on desktop, mobile and XR. Research pipelines now
demonstrate credible volumetric human capture from portable rigs in unprepared
environments — meaning a Laamb arène or a kalari pit, not only a laboratory.
Heritage institutions are adopting Gaussian splatting for sites; applying it to
living combat performance is close to unclaimed.

## 4. The terms we hold ourselves to

This is the part we would most welcome scrutiny on.

**Practitioners are co-authors, not subjects.** Every capture session runs under
a signed release with revenue-share terms. Masters are credited by name on their
accession, in the same position an author would be.

**Communities hold a veto.** Material comes out on request, without argument.
Where knowledge is restricted — the kapu around Hawaiian lua, the tapu around
Māori mau rākau, sacred dimensions of initiation rites — the museum records that
a restriction exists and does not attempt to work around it.

**Rights travel with the file.** Every asset carries a machine-readable record
of licence, attribution and verification date. Nothing enters the collection
without one.

**We publish what we get wrong.** Two widely-circulated "African martial arts",
Maratabeen and Kayti, turned out on investigation to be fabrications propagating
between content farms from a single 2004 forum post. Rather than quietly drop
them, the museum carries a card documenting the finding, and represents Morocco
and Kenya through verified traditions instead. A museum that records what is not
real is more trustworthy about what is.

## 5. What we are asking for

Not a data transfer. Depending on the partner, one of:

- **Method transfer** — guidance on capture and annotation protocol, and on the
  consent architecture that should sit around it.
- **Exhibition** — an institution's material presented as a wing, on that
  institution's framing and terms, with traffic sent back to them.
- **Joint capture** — a shared programme to make the African kinetic record,
  with practitioner agreements built in from the start rather than retrofitted.
- **Licensing** — for datasets whose non-commercial terms currently prevent
  public display, the rights-holder's preferred terms.
