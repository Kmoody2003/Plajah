**Subject:** African martial arts and the kinetic record — a possible complement to HKMALA

Dear Professor Kenderdine,

I have spent the past weeks building a digital museum of world martial arts, and
HKMALA has been the reference standing behind every decision in it. I am writing
because I think there may be a complement worth discussing.

The museum holds fifty traditions across five wings — African, Asian, European,
American and Pacific — each with cited scholarship and a rights-cleared media
layer, on Plajah, a creator platform. Two parts of it are relevant to you. One is
a plate room built on our own copy of Newberry's 1893 Beni Hasan survey, where a
visitor reads the wrestling registers of Tomb 15 at scan resolution, left to
right, the way the wall was painted to be read. The other is a Motion Lab: a
browser player that streams real capture data, currently CMU Graphics Lab
fighting sequences, built on the conviction your work demonstrated — that
technique is kinetic data, and a still image cannot carry it.

The gap I keep running into is this. While assembling the African wing, I could
not find a motion dataset — skeletal or volumetric — of Dambe, Laamb, Engolo,
Nguni stick fighting, Musangwe, Moraingy, Evala or Nuba wrestling. Not a thin
one. None. The written record exists, largely thanks to Desch-Obi; the video
record exists but is scattered; the kinetic record has never been made. HKMALA
established both the method and the standard for making exactly that kind of
record, and it has been applied to Southern Chinese arts and, as far as I can
tell, nowhere else.

So my question is really about whether the method travels, and whether you would
have any interest in seeing it travel.

**Three things I would value your view on, in ascending order of ambition:**

1. **Method.** Whether your capture and annotation protocol is something you
   would be willing to see adapted for African traditions, and what you would
   warn us about — particularly around practitioner consent and community
   custodianship, where the International Guoshu Association's model seems to me
   the part most worth copying and the part most often skipped.

2. **Exhibition.** Whether HKMALA material might ever sit inside a public
   museum layer of this kind — presented on your terms, with your framing, as a
   wing rather than as content. Our platform is a distribution surface; we are
   not looking to hold your archive, only to show it well and send people back
   to you.

3. **Joint capture.** Whether a collaboration on the African record is
   conceivable — HKMALA's method, our access and funding, practitioner
   agreements with revenue-share and co-authorship built in from the start
   rather than retrofitted.

I am aware that the third is a large question from a stranger, which is why it
is last. Even a conversation about the first would be worth a great deal to us,
and I would be glad to travel or to meet online at whatever length suits you.

I have attached a short proposal setting out the capture programme we have
specced and the rights posture the museum runs on, so you can see the terms we
hold ourselves to before deciding whether the conversation is worth your time.

With genuine admiration for the archive,

Kenneth Moody
